How to read this book

/

This is a research edition, not a manual. The five chapters move from the simplest unit of communication to the most complicated: a message is sent, noise interferes, the page tries to catch what arrives, the medium imposes its grammar, and finally the reader completes the meaning. Each chapter opens with a single dataviz spread that stages the chapter's central claim before the body text takes over.

The grid is twelve columns; the typography is two faces and one italic. Where you see a thin red rule, the page is making a load-bearing claim and the design is asking you to slow down. Sidenotes in pale grey expand on the body without interrupting it. Captions in monospaced type are technical — they tell you what the diagram is, who made it, and where it came from.

If you are reading for argument, follow the body text from chapter to chapter and let the diagrams confirm what the prose has already said. If you are reading for design, start with the chapter openers and the dataviz spreads — they carry the book's visual logic in compressed form. If you are reading to disagree, the marginal notes are where the publication answers itself.

The publication is bilingual where it counts. Two techniques alternate across the multilingual proposal at the back: weight-layering for body text, overprint for chapter titles, and cell rotation for the principles grid. Each is explained where it is first deployed.

→ THE CHAIN, AS A DIAGRAM, AT P. 10 · 11 ////

A model
worth breaking

/

A message is never a single object. It is a chain, and like every chain it is only as strong as the weakest link. Pettersson opens Message Design with this observation, and the rest of the book unfolds from it. To communicate, he writes, is to set up an interplay between two or more persons — an interplay that depends on signals being produced, transmitted, received and deciphered. Each verb is a different kind of work. Each can fail.

The dominant model for that chain is still the one Claude Shannon and Warren Weaver published in 1949. A source selects a message. A transmitter encodes it as a signal. The signal travels through a channel. A receiver decodes it. A destination — a reader, a viewer, a listener — receives what arrives.

Five stages, one straight line, drawn at a Bell Labs desk to describe transmitted data, not human conversation. Pettersson is careful to mark this provenance: the model was about telephony, not telephones; about quantitative measurement of transmitted bits, not about meaning. It had nothing to do with us.

And yet it has shaped seventy years of thinking about communication, and almost as long for design. Wilbur Schramm adapted the diagram in 1954 by adding overlap: for any signal to register, he argued, the experience of the sender and the experience of the receiver must share some territory. Without that overlap, encoding fails. The message is sent but never decoded.

In 1983 Pettersson himself revised the picture, naming the variables that interfere with reception — cultural and social status, mood, memory, experience, the cognitive processes a person brings to the moment.

Signals are
choices

/

What stays useful from Shannon and Weaver is the discipline of naming the stages. A designer who can point to where a message is failing — at the encoding, in the channel, at the decoding — has already done half the work.

What does not survive translation is the assumption that any of these stages is neutral. None of them is. Every stage carries decisions, and every decision carries consequence. A typeface is a signal. A line break is a signal. A red rule above a pull quote is a signal. None of them are content. All of them shape what content becomes.

01SIGNAL
//// PULL QUOTE //// MEANING ////
Meaning is not delivered. It is rebuilt by the reader, with materials the designer has chosen — and the reader's culture, mood and attention are part of the message before the message is opened.

Pettersson's expansion of the model is small but consequential. Where Shannon and Weaver describe a signal as a measurable disturbance, Pettersson treats it as a deliberate act. Signals are choices. The designer is not a transparent conduit between source and receiver. The designer is the encoder, and the encoding has already happened by the time the reader opens the page.

What the rest of this book argues is that every design decision — typeface, hierarchy, pace, ground colour, the silence between words — is a signal that shapes how the message will be reconstructed at the other end. None of these are neutral. All of them can be used well or badly.

01SIGNAL

What the channel keeps,
what it loses

/

Shannon's 1949 paper is precise about one thing in particular. Between the transmitter and the receiver lies the channel — wire, paper, screen, air — and into the channel come perturbations. He calls them noise. In an electrical sense the term is exact: stray currents, atmospheric static, signal degradation across distance.

The transmitter sends a clean wave; the receiver measures something messier; the difference is loss. Shannon's contribution was to model this loss mathematically and to describe how, in principle, a careful encoding could recover the original signal even after the channel had eaten part of it.

The metaphor migrates. In design and editorial practice, the channel is no longer a wire. It is the printed page, the crowded feed, the grey afternoon train, the reader's tired eye. Each of these channels has its own losses.

A WIDER DEFINITION

Pettersson and the writers who follow him argue, gently, that Shannon's category is too narrow for human communication. Noise is not only what the wire does to the wave. It is also what the receiver does to the message before perceiving it: the assumptions brought to a sentence, the cultural references missed, the formatting that signals important in one country and clearance sale in another.

Noise is what you wrote in an industry vocabulary that the reader does not own. Noise is the third paragraph after a difficult morning. Noise is everything that reduces the alignment between intention and reception, regardless of where the reduction happens.

16
01SIGNAL

FOUR PLACES IT ENTERS

/

For the purposes of design it is useful to sort noise by where it occurs in the chain. Physical noise is a property of the medium itself: the misregistered ink, the pixelation of an undersampled image, the glare on a phone screen at noon.

Semantic noise is a property of the language: the jargon, the ambiguous referent, the false cognate, the metaphor that does not travel. Psychological noise belongs to the receiver: divided attention, fatigue, prior beliefs that filter what arrives, the motivated reading that hears what it expects.

Channel noise — the trickiest category — belongs to the medium's grammar: every channel has a logic that the message must accept, and every refusal of that logic is a friction the reader pays for.

These four are not exhaustive. They are a working set. Pettersson is not interested in taxonomy for its own sake; he is interested in the designer's leverage over loss. The taxonomy exists because each kind of noise is reduced by a different intervention. Physical noise is reduced by production. Semantic noise by editing. Psychological noise by sequence and pacing. Channel noise by knowing, in advance, which channel you are using and what it will not carry.

The real argument of Message Design enters here. The point is not that noise can be eliminated — it cannot — but that the designer's first job is to know which noise is operating. Most failures of communication are not failures of effort. They are failures of address: the designer reduces the wrong noise, and the message fails for reasons the designer was never tracking.

02NOISE

The honest
doubling

/

Shannon's mathematical insight, the one designers care about, is not that noise exists. It is that noise is predictable — and that a sender who anticipates loss can build redundancy into the signal so that what arrives, even partial, still carries the message.

In information theory the term has a precise meaning: redundancy is the portion of the signal that exceeds what is strictly required to specify the content. In English the redundancy is famously high. You can delete most of the vowels in this sentence and still recover it. You can also lose every fifth word and still get the gist. The language has built-in slack, and the slack is the safety margin against the channel.

For designers, redundancy is the structural answer to the problem of noise. The page is a noisy channel; the reader is a noisy decoder; therefore the design must oversend.

WHAT REDUNDANCY ACTUALLY LOOKS LIKE

On a well-designed page, redundancy is not repetition. It is the same information delivered through more than one channel. A heading announces the topic; the first sentence restates it; the page number locates it; the running header reminds you which chapter you are in; a marginal note clarifies a term; the typography itself encodes hierarchy without needing to label it.

None of this is decoration. Each element is a redundancy — a second path for the message to arrive — and each is sized to a kind of noise. The running header defends against the reader who flips to the page directly. The marginal note defends against the reader who skipped the introduction. The hierarchy defends against the eye that scans before it reads.

02NOISE

THE REPETITION DEVICES

/

Editorial design has a small, well-known set of redundancy devices. The pull quote restates the page's argument in a different size and weight, so a reader who skims lands on the claim. The caption beneath an image restates what the image is showing, so the reader who looks but does not read the body still leaves with the point.

The folio repeats the page number from the running header, so the reader navigating from the table of contents has two ways to find the place. Every cross-reference, every see page 27, every chapter number on a recto and a verso — these are redundancy. They cost almost nothing in space; they buy a lot of resilience against a distracted reader.

The diagram above makes the idea visible. The same word printed at three intensities fades from emphatic to almost lost. If the channel is clean, any of the three suffices. If the channel is noisy, only the strongest reaches the reader.

A design that ships only the 30% version is a design that has trusted the channel; a design that ships all three has not. There is a limit to this argument. Redundancy past a point becomes its own noise — the page shouts so much that no signal stands out. The discipline of design is knowing which sentences earn the second voice and which do not.

02NOISE

After the flood

/

The page opposite is what Pettersson's contemporaries call information overload — though the term predates the digital era by decades. Bertram Gross used it in 1964 to describe the condition of a manager who receives more reports than can be read. Alvin Toffler popularised it in 1970, before the personal computer existed.¹

The condition is not new and it is not specific to screens. It is what happens when the redundancy that defends against noise has tipped into noise of its own kind. A reader presented with too many simultaneous signals does not slow down and read carefully — the reader stops reading and starts filtering.² Filtering uses different cognitive equipment from reading. It is faster, shallower, and biased toward whatever the reader already expected to find.

The first casualty is nuance. The second is the part of the message the reader did not already agree with. The design failure that produces overload is not usually deliberate. It tends to come from a familiar mistake: the designer treats every element as equally important, and so every element is given the same visual weight.³

The remedy is hierarchy, but hierarchy is the difficult kind of restraint. It requires choosing what does not get the largest type, the brightest colour, the top of the page.⁴ Most overloaded pages are evidence not of bad design but of unresolved arguments about importance.

Pettersson's term for the antidote is relevance. Show only what serves the message; everything else is noise that the page itself is generating.⁵ The question is not what could go on the page. The question is what the reader, two minutes from now, needs to remember.

03FRAME

A vocabulary,
not a recipe

/

The sixteen principles are not a checklist. They are a vocabulary — a set of words a designer can use to describe what a page is doing, and to notice what it is failing to do. Pettersson borrowed them from a longer history of design pedagogy, condensed them, and named them with the smallest possible verbs. Each is one principle. None is a rule.

The temptation, when reading a list like this, is to apply every principle to every page. That is not the argument. The argument is the opposite: a page applies the principles its content needs and ignores the rest. A page that applies all sixteen evenly has applied none of them. The principles are tools, not commandments.

HOW THEY FAIL

Each principle has a failure mode that looks, at a glance, like its opposite. Simplicity, applied dogmatically, becomes austerity — a page so stripped that the reader cannot find the entrance. Hierarchy, applied dogmatically, becomes melodrama — a page so loudly tiered that the reader cannot trust the headline. Contrast, applied dogmatically, becomes noise.

The discipline is to know when to stop. A principle is well-applied when the reader does not notice it. A principle is poorly applied when the principle becomes the message.

What follows is a closer look at two of the sixteen — Simplicity and Emphasis — chosen because they are the two most often cited and the two most often misread.

Pettersson treats the sixteen as a working vocabulary. He returns to them throughout the book, naming which principle is in operation and which is being violated. The grammar is descriptive, not prescriptive. To say a page lacks proximity is not to condemn it; it is to point at where the failure of communication is taking place.

The same diagnostic mode applies whether the page is a magazine spread, a poster, a wayfinding sign, or a screen interface.

The thing the principles share is that they are answers to the same underlying question — how do you make the message arrive? — and they answer it from different angles. Some are structural (alignment, structure, proximity). Some are perceptual (contrast, emphasis, hierarchy). Some are economic (simplicity, economy, relevance). Each is an account of what gets in the reader's way when it is missing.

//// PRINCIPLE 01 ////

Simplicity

/

Eliminate the unnecessary so the necessary may speak.

Simplicity is the principle most often confused with minimalism. They are different things. Minimalism is a style — an outcome that looks bare. Simplicity is a procedure — the discipline of removing what is not load-bearing. A page that has been simplified can look dense, even crowded, if every element is doing work. A page that has been minimised can look bare and yet still be cluttered, because the few elements left are not the right ones.

Müller-Brockmann's grid covers and Beck's diagram of the London Underground are the two examples Pettersson cites most. Each is dense — every line and label is necessary — and yet each reads as simple. They were arrived at by elimination, not by reduction. The difference is what the designer kept.

//// PRINCIPLE 03 ////

Emphasis

/

Where the eye lands first, the mind agrees.

Emphasis is the principle that decides where the reader looks. It is harder to use than it seems, because emphasis is a relation, not an attribute. A bold sentence is not emphatic; a bold sentence among regular sentences is. Emphasis is created by difference, and the difference must be strong enough to be perceived without being so strong that it breaks the page.

Lubalin's typographic emphasis — letters at scale, words as image — is the canonical reference. Each Lubalin headline is a study in what difference can do. The body text recedes; the headline does the work of orienting the reader.

Used badly, emphasis becomes melodrama: every paragraph in bold, every other word in italics, the whole page screaming. Used well, it is invisible — the reader's eye travels in the order the designer intended.

No medium is innocent. Every channel imposes its own grammar on the message — and the designer's job is to know which grammar is in operation before deciding what to say.

The matrix opposite is comparative. Each medium has a profile, not a score. Text is unmatched for clarity and retention but weak for emotion. Image is the inverse: emotionally rich, but reliant on caption for clarity. Audio carries more emotion than text and less than image; it retains poorly because the listener cannot scan back. Video is the loudest channel — high emotion, high retention, but also the highest noise risk because every channel decision (cut, sound mix, framing) is a place the message can fail.

The argument is not that one medium is better. The argument is that each medium has a grammar the message must accept. A statistical claim sent through emotion will be doubted. A grief sent through a chart will feel cold. The discipline is matching message to medium, not asserting one over the other.

Each medium
has a grammar

/

A medium is not a passive vessel. It is an active grammar — a set of decisions it has already made about what it can carry and what it cannot. Choosing a medium is choosing those decisions. To send a message through video is to accept that the viewer will not scan back, that the cut between shots is itself a sentence, that the soundtrack is part of the meaning whether you wanted it to be or not.

To send the same message through a printed page is to accept the opposite: the reader can return, the reader controls the pace, but the reader will not hear inflection. What was urgent in the speaker's voice flattens into prose. The text will need to rebuild what the voice carried for free.

CHOOSING IS PRIORITY-SETTING

Pettersson's argument is not that designers should master every medium. It is that designers should know what each medium is for. Text retains best — it is the channel of last resort when the message must survive. Image emotes best — it is the channel of first encounter when the message must land. Graphic clarifies best — it is the channel for relations the prose cannot draw.

Audio and video, the two with the highest noise risk, are also the two with the highest reach. The trade is real. The designer who chooses video gains emotion and immediacy, and pays in clarity and retention. The designer who chooses text gains clarity and retention, and pays in emotion and reach.

41
04TOOLS

Multimodal,
not maximal

/

Most contemporary messages combine media. A magazine spread carries text and image. A documentary carries video, audio, and on-screen text. A presentation carries spoken word, projected image, and live presence. The designer is no longer choosing one medium; the designer is orchestrating several.

The risk in multimodal design is the assumption that more channels equals more impact. It does not. Adding a channel without a job for it just adds noise. A documentary with on-screen text that repeats the narration is louder, not clearer. A spread with a caption that paraphrases the image is wasting the caption's job.

EACH CHANNEL DOES SOMETHING THE OTHERS CANNOT

The discipline of multimodal design is to assign each channel a job that only that channel can do. The narration carries the argument. The image carries the emotion. The on-screen text carries the technical detail the narration cannot pause to give. Each layer is necessary. Each layer is unique.

A design that achieves this feels effortless. The reader, the viewer, the listener arrives at the message and does not notice the construction. The construction has done its job — which is to disappear.

This is harder than it sounds. Most multimodal failures come from designers using extra channels for confidence rather than for content. The cure is the diagnostic question one more time: what does this layer do that the others cannot? If the answer is "make sure they got it," the layer is noise.

05MEANING

The reader
closes the loop

/

Pettersson's last argument, and the one this book has been moving toward, is that meaning is not delivered. It is rebuilt, by the reader, with materials the designer has chosen. The Shannon-Weaver chain stops at the receiver. Pettersson's chain continues — into the receiver's culture, mood, prior experience, expectations. The work of design ends when the page is printed; the work of meaning begins when the page is opened.

This is not a romantic claim. It is a practical one. If the reader's culture is part of the construction, then the designer's failure to anticipate that culture is a failure of design — not a failure of the reader. A page that works in one country and fails in another has not "failed to translate". It has failed to design for the reader the second country contains.

RECEPTION IS NOT PASSIVE

To read is to construct. The reader who scans your headline is making a hypothesis about your subject. The reader who returns to your pull quote is testing that hypothesis. The reader who skips your sidebar is deciding the sidebar was not for them. Each of these acts is interpretation, and each happens before the body text begins.

The designer's leverage is not to control interpretation — that is impossible — but to weight it. To design is to choose which hypotheses are easy to form and which are hard. To design well is to weight the easy ones toward the message and the hard ones toward distraction.

The reader will still close the loop. The reader will always close the loop. The only question is what materials the reader had to work with.
